I have lived in Seoul long enough that the subway feels like an extension of my own living room, but I still remember the first month. Every transfer felt like a small exam I had not studied for. I would stand in the middle of a transfer hall, turning in a slow circle, trying to match the color on the wall to the color on my phone, while a thousand people flowed around me like I was a rock in a river.

Nobody stopped. Nobody glared. That is the first thing to know about the Seoul subway. It is built for people who already know where they are going, and it is surprisingly forgiving once you learn its three or four basic habits.

The second thing to know is that you do not need to understand the whole system. You need one card, one app, and the willingness to get off at the wrong station once. The wrong station is how you actually learn the city. I have done it more times than I can count, and every time the fix was the same: cross the platform, wait four minutes, and ride back the way you came.

## The card comes first

Before anything else, get a T-money card. You can buy one at almost any convenience store, including the ones at Incheon Airport, for around 4,000 to 5,000 KRW, and then you top it up with cash or a card at the same counter. The card works on the subway, on city buses, and at many convenience stores, so it quietly becomes your wallet for the small stuff.

Tap it on the reader as you enter the gates, tap it again as you leave, and that is the whole ceremony. The fare comes off automatically, and a transfer between subway and bus within about half an hour costs you little or nothing extra. That small kindness adds up over a week of sightseeing.

A single subway ride usually runs about 1,400 to 1,600 KRW on the card, with the price rising a little for very long distances. I am giving you a range because fares get adjusted every year or two, and the exact number matters less than the shape of it: a ride across the city costs less than a coffee. Keep the card topped up with 20,000 to 30,000 KRW at a time and you will stop thinking about money entirely, which is the point. Prices in this guide are ranges, last checked November 2026.

If you lose the card, it is gone, and so is whatever balance was on it. I keep mine in my phone case, in the same slot every day, because the one time I dropped it in a jacket pocket I spent a whole afternoon retracing my steps through three stations. Treat it like cash, because that is what it is.

## Reading the station

A Seoul subway station is a small underground neighborhood, and the signs are doing more work than they first appear to do. Every line has a color and a number, and every station has a number of its own, so you can navigate by numbers alone even if you cannot read a word of Korean. Line 2 is green and runs in a big circle around the city. Line 4 is light blue. Line 5 is purple.

When someone tells you to meet them at Hongik University Station, what they really mean is Line 2, station 239, or the AREX line if you are coming from the airport. The exit number matters as much as the station name. Exit 9 at Hongik University puts you in the middle of the Hongdae streets. Exit 1 puts you somewhere else entirely, and you will spend ten minutes walking back.

The transfer halls are the part that worries people, and they should not. Follow the signs that say Transfer with an arrow, and keep walking. Some transfers are short, a single flight of stairs. Some, like the walk between lines at Seoul Station or Dongdaemun History and Culture Park, are long enough that you will wonder if you took a wrong turn. You did not. The tunnels are just long, and they are full of shops, which is how the city quietly sells you socks and phone cases while you change trains. Give yourself ten minutes for a transfer you have never done before, and five once you know the route.

One habit that saves real time: stand on the right side of the escalator and walk on the left, the way everyone else does without thinking about it. The other habit is to check the direction before you step onto the platform, not after. Every platform sign shows the terminal station in that direction, so if you are heading toward Hongik University on Line 2, you want the train going toward Hongdae, not the one going toward City Hall. I still say the destination out loud to myself when I am tired. It has saved me more than once.

## Inside the car

The inside of a Seoul subway car is quieter than you might expect. People talk softly or not at all, and most of them are looking at their phones. The seats at the ends of each car are priority seats, marked for elderly passengers, pregnant women, and people with disabilities, and visitors are expected to leave them empty even when the car is crowded.

I have seen tourists sit there without realizing, and nobody says anything, but the people standing around them notice. The regular seats are fair game, and if the car is full, standing is normal and nobody minds.

The doors chime, a voice announces the station in Korean and English, and the station name is written on the platform wall in large letters as you pull in. If you miss your stop, stay calm. Get off at the next one, cross to the other platform, and ride back. The trains come every few minutes during the day, and a missed stop costs you five minutes at most. The only time a missed stop truly hurts is the last train, which brings me to the one rule that matters after dark.

## Rush hour is a real thing

The subway runs from about 05:30 until around midnight, and for most of the day it is comfortable. Twice a day it is not. From about 08:00 to 09:30 in the morning and 18:00 to 19:30 in the evening, the cars fill up fast, and at the busiest stations you may watch one full train go by before you can squeeze onto the next. It is not dangerous, and it is not rude, but it is not pleasant with a backpack and a suitcase, and it is the worst time to be figuring out a transfer for the first time.

My honest advice is to plan around it. Eat a slow breakfast and start sightseeing at ten. Or start very early, before eight, and be already somewhere beautiful when the rush begins. If you must travel at rush hour, travel light, keep your bag in front of you, and let the crowd carry you toward the doors. The push is gentle. Nobody is angry. Everyone is just trying to get to work.

Late at night, the rhythm flips. The last trains leave the terminal stations around midnight, and the exact time depends on the line and the station, so check the last train on Naver Map before you settle into a late dinner. I have run for a last train exactly once, down a very long transfer tunnel, and I do not recommend it as a holiday memory. These days I check the time before I order a second drink.

## The app that actually works

Everyone will tell you to install Naver Map, and everyone is right. Google Maps works poorly for walking and transit directions in Korea, and the gap is not small. Naver Map gives you the platform, the direction, the transfer walking time, and the exact exit number, in English, and it recalculates the moment you miss a turn. It is the single most useful thing you can put on your phone before you land. KakaoMap is the other good option, and either one beats guessing.

What Naver Map will not do is hold your hand through a taxi ride, and that is where Kakao T comes in. It is the taxi app most Koreans use, it has an English setting, and it lets you call a car, see the fare estimate, and pay by card without negotiating anything with the driver. Download it and set it up while you are still at home, because setting up payment in a taxi at 1 a.m. is nobody's idea of fun.

## When to take a taxi

The subway wins almost every daytime trip in Seoul. It is cheaper, it is often faster, and it never gets stuck in traffic. But there are three situations where I take a taxi without a second thought. The first is after midnight, when the subway has stopped and the night buses are the only other option. The second is when I am carrying something heavy or awkward, like a suitcase on the way to the airport, or shopping bags after a long day in Myeongdong. The third is when three or four of us are traveling together, because the fare splits nicely and the door-to-door trip beats four subway fares and a ten-minute walk.

A taxi ride in Seoul starts at around 4,800 KRW on the meter, and a short hop across a neighborhood usually lands somewhere between 6,000 and 12,000 KRW, depending on traffic.

{{< figure src="images/photos/seoul-09.webp" alt="Orange Seoul taxi, front view" caption="An orange Seoul taxi, the regular kind you will use most. Photo: U2em, CC BY-SA 3.0" >}}

A ride from central Seoul to the airport runs roughly 60,000 to 80,000 KRW, more late at night, when a surcharge of 20 to 40 percent applies roughly between 10 p.m. and 4 a.m. You can pay by card in nearly every taxi, and tipping is not expected. If a driver ever quotes you a flat price instead of using the meter for a normal city ride, just walk away and call another one through the app.

The taxis themselves come in a few colors, and the color tells you something. The orange and white ones are the regular taxis you will use most. The black ones with a gold stripe are deluxe taxis, nicer cars and higher fares, which you do not need. The silver ones are often older cars run by individual drivers. In practice, any taxi with its red light on is available, and you can wave one down on any main street. At night in Hongdae or Itaewon, the street hails get competitive, and that is when the app earns its keep.

## Where to eat near the stations

You do not need to plan meals around the subway, but the stations put you next to the food anyway.

- **Hongik University Station, Exit 9.** The Hongdae streets start here. Street stalls and small shops sell snacks for roughly 3,000-8,000 KRW each. Eat standing up and keep walking.
- **Myeongdong Station.** The food street starts a few minutes from the gates. Items run 3,000-10,000 KRW. Share, so you can try more.
- **Jongno 5-ga Station, Line 1, Exit 8.** Gwangjang Market is right there. A full breakfast of pancakes and noodles runs about 10,000-15,000 KRW. Bring cash.

## The mistakes I still see, and the ones I made

The most common mistake is treating the subway like a tourist attraction instead of a tool. People plan their whole day around the lines and then get frustrated when a transfer takes twelve minutes. Plan your day around neighborhoods instead. Pick one area, walk it properly, and let the subway be the twenty-minute hop between areas. The city rewards the walking. The best meal I had last month was in an alley I found because I got off one station early by accident.

{{< figure src="images/photos/seoul-05.webp" alt="Gyeongui Line Forest Trail street near Hongdae in daytime" caption="The Gyeongui Line Forest Trail near Hongdae, where getting off a stop early pays off. Photo: Christian Bolz, CC BY-SA 4.0" >}}

The second mistake is over-preparing. You do not need a rail pass, a tour, or a printed map. You need the card, the app, and a charged phone. Seoul's transit system is one of the most forgiving in the world for a first-time visitor, and the staff at the station information desks will help you in English if you ask. I have watched a station attendant walk a lost traveler all the way to the right platform, and I have been that traveler.

The third mistake is mine, and I still make it: assuming the last train will wait. It will not. If you are out late, check the time, and if the subway is gone, take the taxi and enjoy the empty streets. Some of my favorite views of this city have come from the back seat of a taxi at 1 a.m., crossing the Han River with the lights on the water and nobody else on the bridge.
