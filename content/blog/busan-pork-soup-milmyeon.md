Busan eats differently from Seoul. The signature meal here is not barbecue or a palace-district soup. It is a bowl of pork and rice soup eaten at eight in the morning, or at midnight after a long night, in a room full of shift workers and fishermen and nobody in a hurry. I have eaten gukbap at both of those hours, and the bowl tastes the same. That is the point of it.

This guide covers two dishes and four restaurants. Dwaeji gukbap is pork-bone soup ladled over rice with slices of boiled pork. Milmyeon is Busan's own cold noodle, chewy wheat-and-starch noodles in an icy broth, born when wartime refugees could not get buckwheat and cooked with wheat flour instead. Both are cheap. Both are eaten all year. And both are the fastest way to understand what Busan tastes like.

The soup arrives plain on purpose. You season it yourself at the table with salted shrimp and chopped chives. Some shops also put out doenjang. Taste it first. Then add a little. Then a little more. The bowl is yours to finish, and nobody at the next table is watching how you do it.


## Songjeong Samdae Gukbap: the Seomyeon anchor

Songjeong Samdae Gukbap has stood in Seomyeon's dwaeji-gukbap alley since 1946, and it is now run by the third generation of the same family. The address is 33 Seomyeon-ro 68beon-gil, about 200 meters from Exit 1 of Seomyeon Station, and it is open 24 hours. The alley barely closes. At 3 a.m. the room is still half full.

The broth is what sets it apart. Most gukbap shops boil pork bones. This one boils beef leg bones alongside the pork, which gives the soup a milkier color and a richer taste than the shops nearby. A bowl of dwaeji gukbap is ₩9,500. The sundae and offal versions are the same price. The boiled-pork set, with a plate of sliced pork on the side, is ₩12,000. The portions are generous, and the dining room is clean and comfortable in a way the alley's older shops are not.

The sticky-rice sundae is the one thing I would skip. It starts at ₩11,000, and it is packed so densely with rice that it eats like a second bowl. Order the plain dwaeji gukbap, or the suyuk set if you are properly hungry. The shop takes cards. It never closes, so it works as a first breakfast after an early arrival or a last meal before a late train. The wait at lunch is short even when the room looks full, because nobody lingers over a bowl of soup.

## Haeundae Wonjo Halmae Gukbap: beef soup by the beach

Haeundae's old soup house is a different animal. Haeundae Wonjo Halmae Gukbap serves beef gukbap, not pork, and it has been doing it for nearly 50 years near Haeundae Beach. The address to use is 33 Gunam-ro 21beon-gil, a short walk from Haeundae Station toward the sea. There is a similarly named shop nearby at number 27, so check that your map pin shows number 33 and the Korean name 해운대원조할매국밥 before you set out.

The soup is clear and mildly spicy, cooked with bean sprouts and radish, and it tastes lighter than the milky pork broth in Seomyeon. Beef gukbap is ₩9,500, and the seonji version with ox-blood curd is ₩9,500 too. The ttarro version, with the rice served on the side instead of in the soup, is ₩10,000. It is a good breakfast after a morning walk on the beach, and it is the bowl to order if pork soup sounds too heavy before noon.

The hours are the one thing I could not pin down cleanly. Busan's multilingual menu service lists 08:00 to 21:00 daily, while other listings show near-round-the-clock hours with a Wednesday closure. Treat the hours as unconfirmed and check the map listing before you go. Some visitors have also reported that the shop only took cash, so bring paper money just in case. The food is worth the small fuss. The admin is not.


## Halmae Gaya Milmyeon: the easy first bowl

Milmyeon needs a different kind of attention from soup. The noodles are wheat and starch, yellowish and chewy, served in a chilled clear broth under a sheet of ice. It is Busan's summer dish, but the shops serve it all year, and on a cold day the contrast of icy noodles and a warm room is half the pleasure.

Halmae Gaya Milmyeon is the easiest first stop. It has been open since 1974 on Gwangbok-ro in Nampo-dong, a few minutes' walk from Nampo Station and BIFF Square, close to Gukje Market and Jagalchi. It is open 10:30 to 21:30 every day, and the room is big enough that the wait is usually short. The address is 56-14 Gwangbok-ro.

A large milmyeon is ₩9,000. The bibim version with spicy sauce is ₩8,000. The handmade jumbo mandu, four pieces, are ₩5,000, and they are worth ordering. The bibim is the one to try if you like heat, and the plain mul-milmyeon is the one to try first if this is your first bowl. Either way, eat it fast. The noodles go soft as the ice melts, and the shop knows it, which is why nobody lingers.

## Naeho Naengmyeon: where milmyeon began

Naeho Naengmyeon is the bowl with the story. The shop sits in a narrow alley of Uam Market in Uam-dong, Nam-gu, at 17 Uambeonyeong-ro 26beon-gil. It opened in 1952, run by a family from Hungnam who had made naengmyeon there since 1919, and it is widely called the birthplace of milmyeon. The Korea Tourism Organization lists its hours as 10:30 to 19:00, last order 18:30, with no closing day.

Getting there takes more effort than the other stops on this list. Uam-dong is not on the tourist loop, and the alley is narrow enough that you will doubt the map once or twice. That is part of it. The room is old and small, and the noodles are the point. Mul-naengmyeon is the main bowl, and bibim-milmyeon is the spicy one. Seasoned skate is there for the brave. No official source lists current prices, so I will not print a number here. Look at the menu board when you arrive. A bowl of milmyeon in Busan generally runs ₩7,000 to ₩10,000, and this shop sits in that range.

The honest trade-off is the trip itself. If you are spending the day around Nampo-dong, start at Halmae Gaya instead. Save Naeho for a day when you have time to wander, and go before 18:30 so you are not turned away at the door. The history is real, but a bowl of cold noodles is not worth a ruined afternoon of rushing across town.

A simple way to eat all four: start at Songjeong Samdae in Seomyeon in the morning, when the alley is quiet and the broth is fresh. Take the subway to Nampo-dong for milmyeon at Halmae Gaya in the afternoon, and walk the market streets while the ice melts off your chopsticks. Save Haeundae for the evening, with beef gukbap after the beach, and keep Naeho for a second day if you have one. Four bowls, two days, and about ₩40,000 of food that tells you more about Busan than any viewpoint in the city.
