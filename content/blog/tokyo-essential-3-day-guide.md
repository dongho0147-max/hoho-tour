[Tokyo](https://www.klook.com/en-US/city/28-tokyo-things-to-do/?aid=145455) is the easiest big trip from Seoul, and three days is enough to see the two cities it contains. One is the old east side, temples and market streets around Asakusa and Ueno. The other is the new west side, the Shibuya and Shinjuku you already know from photos. The trick is to do one side per day and let the Yamanote loop line do the work.

This guide is written from research, not from a single visit. Routes, times, and prices below come from official pages and recent listings, checked November 2026. Treat prices as reference points, not promises, and double-check the big tickets before you fly.

Two things changed in 2026 that older guides still get wrong. The tax-free system moved to airport refunds on November 1, 2026, so you no longer get the discount at the register. And JR East raised fares in March 2026 for the first time since privatization, so any guide quoting a 150-yen base fare is out of date.

## ✈️ Which airport, and how do you get into the city?

If you are flying from Korea, you will most likely land at Haneda, since the Gimpo-Haneda route is short and frequent. Haneda sits inside the city, and the ride in is cheap. The Tokyo Monorail runs from Terminal 3 to Hamamatsucho in 13 minutes for {{< hl "520 yen" >}}, where you transfer to the Yamanote line. The Keikyu line runs to Shinagawa in about the same time for {{< pr "330 yen" >}}, and some trains continue straight onto the Asakusa subway line.

Narita is farther out, about 60 kilometers east, and the ride costs more. The fastest options are the N'EX to **Tokyo Station** at {{< pr "3,140 yen" >}} in about 53 minutes, and the Keisei Skyliner to Ueno at {{< pr "2,580 yen" >}} ({{< pr "1,280 yen" >}} fare + {{< pr "1,300 yen" >}} liner ticket) in about 41 minutes. From March 13, 2027, the liner ticket increases by {{< pr "200 yen" >}}, making the Skyliner total {{< pr "2,780 yen" >}}. The budget option is the Keisei main line at {{< pr "1,050 yen" >}}, and the TYO-NRT shuttle bus runs to Tokyo Station for {{< pr "1,500 yen" >}} with no reservation needed.

> **🔥 Local tip:** If you fly into Narita on the N'EX and back out within 14 days, ask for the N'EX Tokyo Round Trip Ticket at the airport station. It costs {{< pr "5,200 yen" >}} for foreigners, which is close to half the regular round-trip fare.

## 🚇 How does the subway work, and which pass is worth it?

Tokyo has two subway companies plus JR, and they charge separately. Tokyo Metro runs nine lines, the Toei subway runs four, and JR runs the Yamanote loop above ground. Base fares are small: {{< pr "178 yen" >}} by IC card on both subway companies, and {{< pr "155 yen" >}} on JR since the March 2026 fare revision. An IC card sorts out the transfer math automatically, so get one before your first ride.

For visitors there are two special IC cards. The Welcome Suica and the TOURIST PASMO, launched in 2026, both last 28 days, need no deposit, and cannot be refunded. Regular Suica and PASMO cards went back on sale in March 2025 with a refundable {{< pr "500 yen" >}} deposit, and iPhone users can skip the plastic entirely by adding Suica to Apple Pay. Any of them also pays at convenience stores and vending machines.

If you plan to ride a lot in one day, look at the passes. The Tokyo Metro 24-hour ticket costs {{< hl "700 yen" >}}, covers all nine Metro lines, and pays for itself from the fourth ride. Visitors can also buy the Tokyo Subway Ticket online, which covers both Metro and Toei lines: {{< pr "1,000 yen" >}} for 24 hours, {{< pr "1,500 yen" >}} for 48 hours, and {{< pr "2,000 yen" >}} for 72 hours. The combined Metro and Toei one-day pass costs {{< pr "1,100 yen" >}} at station machines, and the Tokyo Free Ticket at {{< pr "1,720 yen" >}} adds JR lines inside the city plus the Toei buses and the Sakura tram.

## 🗼 Day 1: Senso-ji, Ueno, and the Skytree

Start in Asakusa, the oldest-feeling corner of the city. Senso-ji is free, and the walk from the Kaminarimon gate up the Nakamise shopping street to the main hall is the classic first morning in Tokyo. Go before ten if you can. By midday the street is shoulder to shoulder, and the snack stalls have lines.

From Asakusa it is one stop or a short river-bus ride to Tokyo Skytree. The tower switched to date-based dynamic pricing for visits from April 1, 2026, so the adult ticket for both observation decks starts at {{< pr "3,000 yen" >}} and moves with demand. Buying at the window adds {{< pr "500 yen" >}} per ticket, so book online on the official calendar before you go. On a clear day the view reaches Mount Fuji, and the clearest air is usually in winter and early morning.

If you have energy left, Ueno is two subway stops away. Ameya-Yokocho, the market street under the train tracks, sells everything from dried squid to sneakers, and it is one of the few places in Tokyo where haggling sometimes works.

## 🍣 Where and what to eat

The honest answer is that Tokyo makes it hard to eat badly, and the structured answer is to plan meals by neighborhood. Start one morning at the **Tsukiji Outer Market**, where the old market's food stalls still serve sushi breakfasts and tamagoyaki on sticks. Go early, because many stalls close by early afternoon and a lot of them take Sundays and holidays off. A filling market breakfast runs roughly 2,000 to {{< pr "4,000 yen" >}} depending on how serious the sushi is.

For lunch, two genre stops are worth seeking out. Monjayaki, Tokyo's runny local answer to okonomiyaki, has its own street in Tsukishima, one station from Ginza. Ramen has a basement hall at Tokyo Station called Ramen Street, where several famous shops keep branches, so you can try a top bowl without crossing the city. Most ramen shops take cash only at a ticket machine, and a bowl runs about 1,000 to {{< pr "1,500 yen" >}}.

At night, the west side takes over. Omoide Yokocho by **Shinjuku Station** is a lantern-lit alley of tiny yakitori counters, and Golden Gai nearby packs some two hundred miniature bars into a few lanes. Many Golden Gai bars add a table charge, usually 500 to {{< pr "1,500 yen" >}} per person, and the amount varies by shop, so check the sign at the door before you sit down. Department store basements, called depachika, are the local secret for a high-quality cheap dinner: the same food halls discount sushi and bento in the last hour before closing.

## 🌃 Day 2: Meiji Jingu, Harajuku, and Shibuya at sunset

Start at Meiji Jingu, the forest shrine a minute from **Harajuku Station**, free and quietest before nine. Then walk back through Takeshita Street, the teenage fashion lane famous for crepes, and continue up Omotesando's tree-lined avenue toward Shibuya. The whole walk is about thirty minutes without stops, which is not how anyone actually does it.

Time Shibuya for late afternoon. Watch the scramble crossing from the station, then go up SHIBUYA SKY, the open-air rooftop on top of Shibuya Scramble Square. Online tickets cost {{< pr "2,700 yen" >}} for entry before 3 PM and {{< pr "3,400 yen" >}} after, and sunset slots sell out days ahead, so book when your dates are fixed. Tickets open two weeks in advance at midnight Japan time. One new rule to know: since November 19, 2026, overseas buyers must show an original passport at entry, and the name has to match the booking.

## 🏙️ Day 3: Shinjuku, a free view, and the park that closes on Mondays

Shinjuku is the world's busiest station and a full day on its own. Start at the Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building, where both observatories on the 45th floor are free. On a clear winter morning you can see Mount Fuji from here without paying a yen. Then cross to Shinjuku Gyoen, the city's best-paid park at {{< pr "500 yen" >}}, and the single most useful fact about it is that it closes on Mondays. It opens seven days a week during cherry blossom season and the November chrysanthemum exhibition, but the rest of the year, Monday is a dead day.

For the evening, stay on the west side. Kabukicho and Omoide Yokocho are both a short walk from the station, and the department stores around the east exit run their depachika discounts from about an hour before closing.

## 🎨 If you have a fourth day

Two add-ons earn their time. teamLab Planets in Toyosu is the immersive digital art museum where you wade barefoot through knee-deep water. Adult tickets start from around {{< pr "3,800 yen" >}} and vary by date. Tickets are sold online only for a fixed date and time slot, there are no sales at the door, and weekend slots go early, so book it the week before, not the day before.

The other is **Toyosu Market** itself, where the tuna auctions moved from Tsukiji in 2018. Watching the market from the visitor decks is free, and it pairs naturally with teamLab ten minutes away. The old Tsukiji site, remember, still lives on as the outer market from Day 1's breakfast.

## 🛍️ Shopping: the tax-free rules changed on November 1, 2026

This is the change most older guides miss. Until October 2026, tax-free shops in Japan sold you goods with the consumption tax removed at the register. From November 1, 2026, the system moved to refunds: you pay the full tax-included price in the store, customs confirms at the airport that you are taking the goods out of the country, and the tax comes back to you as a refund.

The practical effect is simple. Keep every receipt, do not use the consumables before you leave, and add twenty to thirty minutes at the airport for the customs check and refund counter. Don Quijote, the drugstores, and the department stores all handle it, and the minimum spend rules still apply, but the money now comes back at the airport, not at the till.

## 💴 Money and the small practical stuff

Japan still runs on more cash than Korea. Cards work at chains, but market stalls, old restaurants, and ramen ticket machines often do not take them, so withdraw yen from a Seven Bank ATM inside any 7-Eleven, which accepts international cards. There is no tipping anywhere, not in taxis, not in restaurants.

Three small things surprise first-timers. Public trash cans barely exist, so carry a small bag for wrappers and expect to take trash back to your hotel. The power is 100 volts with flat two-pin Type A plugs, so Korean round-pin chargers need an adapter, though most modern chargers handle the voltage fine. And register on Visit Japan Web before you fly to get the immigration and customs QR codes, which turns the arrival line into a scan. The {{< pr "1,000 yen" >}} departure tax is already inside your ticket price.

## 🌸 When to come

Cherry blossom season is the famous window, and the official averages say flowering around March 24 and full bloom around March 31. In 2026 it came early, March 19 and March 28, so treat any forecast as a moving target and aim for the last week of March. **Ueno Park**, Shinjuku Gyoen, the Meguro River, and Chidorigafuchi are the classic spots.

The other strong windows are late November for autumn leaves and winter for clear skies and illuminations. Summer is festival season, the Sumida River fireworks ran on July 25 in 2026, but it is hot and humid enough that you will plan your days around air conditioning.

## 🚫 Skip this if...

Skip the Skytree on a cloudy day, because the whole point is the view and the window surcharge is real money. Skip teamLab if you hate timed tickets, because the whole visit runs on a slot you booked a week ago. And skip Golden Gai with a big group, because the bars seat six people and the table charge punishes a crowd.
