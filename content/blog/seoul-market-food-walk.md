There is a way to eat in Seoul that has nothing to do with restaurants. No reservation, no menu, no table. You walk into a market in the morning, hungry, with cash in your pocket, and you let the smell decide. I do this walk a few times a year, usually on a weekday morning when the market belongs to the regulars, and it is still the best food day I know in this city.

It is also the most forgiving. You cannot order wrong in a market. You point, you eat, you keep walking.

The walk I am going to describe starts at Gwangjang Market, winds through the old streets around Jongno, and ends wherever your feet give out, usually in a narrow hanok alley with a coffee in your hand. It takes a morning, it costs less than a single restaurant dinner, and it shows you a Seoul that the shopping districts never will. If you want the full first-trip version of the city, the palaces and the river and the tower, my [Seoul Essential 3-Day Guide](/blog/seoul-essential-3-day-guide/) is the place to start. This walk is what you do on the morning after, when you already know your way around and you want to eat like someone who lives here.

## Go early, go hungry, bring cash

Gwangjang Market opens early, and the food stalls hit their stride by nine or ten in the morning. Come before the tour groups arrive and the market is still waking up: the vendors setting out their trays, the ajummas in their aprons calling out to each other across the aisle, the smell of oil and sesame and something frying that you cannot quite name. That smell is bindaetteok, the mung bean pancake the market is famous for, and you should follow it.

The way it works is simple. You find a stall with a hot griddle and a small crowd, you order a pancake, and you eat it standing at the counter or perched on a plastic stool with everyone else. A pancake runs roughly 5,000 to 8,000 KRW. Add a plate of mayak gimbap, the little seaweed rolls that people call addictive for a reason, for a few thousand won more, and a bowl of knife-cut noodles if you are properly hungry, and you have eaten one of the great breakfasts of the world for about 10,000 to 20,000 KRW per person. Bring cash. Cards work in more and more stalls every year, but the old market still runs on paper money, and the vendors who have been there for decades will appreciate it. I carry 20,000 to 30,000 KRW in small bills for a market morning, and I have never once regretted it. There are ATMs nearby, but the walk to find one is a walk away from the food, and that is the wrong direction.

## The aisle teaches you how to look

The first time I walked Gwangjang properly, I rushed it. I had a list in my head, three dishes, in and out, and I missed the whole point. The market is not a restaurant with a menu. It is a place to look first and eat second.

The fish stall is the best example. Stand in front of it for a minute and really look: the rows of fresh fish on ice, the dried fish hanging overhead, the vendor working with a knife in one hand and a conversation in the other, moving faster than seems possible. That vendor has been doing this since before I was born, and the stall is her whole world. You are not just buying lunch. You are standing in someone's life for a minute.

If raw fish is your thing, Noryangjin Fisheries Wholesale Market is a separate trip, and it is worth its own morning. The octopus there is the whole show.

{{< figure src="images/photos/seoul-14.webp" alt="Raw octopus at Noryangjin Fisheries Wholesale Market, close-up" caption="Raw octopus at Noryangjin Market, up close. Photo: Basile Morin, CC BY-SA 4.0" >}}

The same goes for the fabric section, which surprises everyone. Gwangjang is also one of the largest fabric markets in Korea, and the aisles of silk and hanbok cloth are a whole second market hiding inside the first. You do not need to buy anything. Walk through it after you eat, when your hands are free and your stomach is full, and look at the colors. The pinks and reds and golds are the same colors you will see in the hanbok rentals near the palace, and now you know where the cloth comes from.

## The food you carry with you

Some market food is for eating on the spot, and some is for carrying. Mayak gimbap belongs to the second group, and it is the smartest thing you can buy in this market. The rolls are small, the seaweed is brushed with sesame oil, and they come in a tray you can carry in one hand. I buy a tray to eat while I walk, and I buy a second tray to take home, because they are still good hours later on a [subway ride](/blog/seoul-subway-tmoney-taxi/) or a park bench. A tray runs a few thousand won, and it is the closest thing Seoul has to a perfect walking snack.

The gimbap you see in the market is not the gimbap from a convenience store, and the difference is everything. The rice is warm, the fillings are fresh and cut that morning, and the roll is made by someone who has made ten thousand of them. Look at a cut roll up close: the colors are the point, the yellow of the pickled radish, the orange of the carrot, the green of the spinach, all held together by the dark seaweed. Eat it within the hour if you can. That is when it is best.

## Out of the market, into the alleys

By late morning, the market starts to fill with visitors, and that is my cue to leave. The walk from Gwangjang to the old streets around Jongno takes ten minutes, and it feels like stepping from one century into another and then back again. The alleys here are narrow, the buildings are low, and the shops are the kind that have been selling the same thing for forty years: brassware, paper, tea, herbal medicine.

You will pass a tea house, then a shop selling nothing but scissors, then a tiny restaurant with four tables and a line out the door. The line is the review. Join it.

{{< collage "images/photos/seoul-18.webp|Bosingak Pavilion in Jongno|sunbeams879, CC BY" "images/photos/seoul-17.webp|Jogyesa Temple interior with golden Buddha statues|Severin.stalder, CC BY-SA 3.0" >}}

This is where the walk stops being a food tour and becomes a wander, and that is the point. I never plan this part. I turn down whatever alley looks interesting, I stop when something smells good, and I keep a coffee in my hand for the last stretch. Ikseon-dong, the hanok alley neighborhood a short walk west, is where I usually end up. The old wooden houses there have become cafes and small restaurants, and the lanes are so narrow that two people can barely pass, which is exactly why they are lovely. Sit down, order something warm, and let your feet rest. You have earned it.

{{< figure src="images/photos/seoul-35.webp" alt="Bibimbap in a bowl, rice topped with carrot, mushroom, zucchini, egg strips, meat and gochujang" caption="Bibimbap, the sit-down bowl after the market walk. Photo: Chloe Lim, CC BY 2.0" >}}

A few practical things I have learned the hard way. Eat a small breakfast before you come, not a big one, because you want to be hungry enough to try things but not so hungry that you fill up at the first stall. Wear shoes you can stand in for three hours, because there is almost nowhere to sit in a market and you will be on your feet the whole time. And do not try to do this walk on a Sunday afternoon in peak season, when the aisles are packed shoulder to shoulder. A weekday morning is the market at its best, and it is when the vendors have time to talk to you.

## Where to eat on this walk

Three stalls to look for, all inside Gwangjang, all cash-friendly.

- **Bindaetteok, the mung bean pancake.** Follow the frying smell to a stall with a hot griddle and a small crowd. Roughly 5,000-8,000 KRW. Eat it standing at the counter.
- **Mayak gimbap, the mini seaweed rolls.** A tray runs a few thousand won. Buy one to eat while you walk and a second to take home. Still good hours later.
- **Knife-cut noodles.** The hot bowl that finishes the morning. With the pancake and the gimbap, the whole breakfast lands around 10,000-20,000 KRW per person.

If you want someone to guide you through it the first time, a good guide will get you into stalls you would walk straight past on your own. But honestly, the walk works without one. The market is the guide. Follow the smell, point at what looks good, bring cash, and leave room for the second tray of gimbap.
